# Art Direction — Hybrid 2.5D

**Status:** revised proposal for review  
**Medium:** illustrated 2D characters and worlds staged in shallow 3D space  
**Priority:** quality and clarity over production speed

## 1. Creative identity

The channel should feel like a fast, clever animated explainer told through a recurring character who experiences unusual hypothetical situations. The visuals are direct, expressive, and immediately readable. The viewer should understand the joke, action, or consequence even before narration explains it.

The target is **2D-first 2.5D**, not conventional 3D character animation. Characters are designed as illustrated shapes with strong outlines and simple color regions. Environments are composed from layered illustrated planes, with selective dimensional props, contact shadows, depth separation, and camera movement.

## 2. Reference principles — not asset copying

Use the reference channel to study broad qualities:
- A recurring character anchors the viewer's attention.
- Simple designs allow emotion and action to read instantly.
- Clean backgrounds keep attention on the story beat.
- Poses and expressions change frequently enough to support narration.
- Visual metaphors, props, and quick scene changes explain abstract ideas.
- Humor comes from timing, contrast, and staging—not visual clutter.

Do **not** copy its exact avatar, face, clothing, silhouette, poses, scene layouts, thumbnails, illustrations, or other assets. Our character and graphic language must be independently recognizable.

## 3. Visual pillars

### Character-first storytelling
- One dominant readable action per shot.
- Expressions and gestures should be legible at phone-screen size.
- Keep the character's design simple enough for frequent pose changes.
- Use anticipation, held reactions, and clear follow-through.

### Graphic simplicity
- Favor bold silhouettes, clean contours, and controlled shape language.
- Use a limited number of colors per shot.
- Prefer large readable forms over tiny decorative details.
- Use outlines consistently; vary line weight only to clarify depth or focus.

### Layered depth
- Separate foreground, character/action plane, and background.
- Use shallow depth and parallax to add dimensionality without turning the scene into a 3D game environment.
- Reserve full 3D geometry for props or moments that benefit from rotation, interaction, or perspective.
- Avoid perspective distortion that makes flat characters feel detached from the world.

### Fast visual communication
- Every shot must communicate one beat: setup, reaction, consequence, comparison, or transition.
- Use visual metaphors, diagrams, labels, and simple graphic overlays when they clarify the narration.
- Transitions should be motivated by the story, not added as decoration.

## 4. Character rendering language

- Characters are illustrated, camera-facing or near-camera-facing assets.
- Prefer 2D cutout rigs with separate head, torso, upper/lower limbs, hands, and facial controls.
- Preserve a consistent outline and shadow treatment.
- Avoid photoreal skin, complex physically based shaders, and detailed anatomy.
- Use subtle 2.5D volume only where it improves readability: layered facial parts, simple bevels, controlled cast shadows, and selective dimensional props.

## 5. Environment language

- Build environments as designed graphic sets, not realistic architectural simulations.
- Use simple geometry, clear perspective, and a few identifying props.
- Background detail must remain subordinate to the character.
- Reuse locations through layout variants, lighting changes, and prop swaps.
- Use foreground framing and background layers to create depth.

## 6. Color and material

Proposed base palette:
- Warm paper: `#F4EFE5`
- Ink: `#252B33`
- Deep teal: `#286B70`
- Ochre: `#E5A442`
- Coral: `#D96C55`
- Mist blue: `#AFC7C8`

Use warm neutral backgrounds as a calm base, dark ink for linework, teal as a recurring clothing/world color, and ochre/coral as selective story accents. A shot should normally use a small subset rather than every palette color.

Materials should read as illustrated matte surfaces. Avoid glossy plastic, noisy textures, excessive gradients, and generic cinematic bloom.

## 7. Camera and motion

- Default to orthographic or weak-perspective staging.
- Use simple pans, pushes, pullbacks, and parallax with clear motivation.
- Keep character scale and screen position consistent across connected shots.
- Use close-ups for meaningful reactions; wide shots for context and visual punchlines.
- Animate holds and pose changes deliberately. Do not keep every layer moving constantly.
- Camera movement must never make captions, faces, or important props difficult to read.

## 8. Typography and graphic overlays

- Use one primary sans-serif family and a consistent hierarchy.
- On-screen text should be short and meaningful.
- Labels, arrows, counters, diagrams, and UI elements should share the same line weight and palette.
- Avoid dense paragraphs on screen; narration carries the detail.

## 9. What this style is not

- Not photorealistic 3D.
- Not generic low-poly animation.
- Not flat slides with unrelated stock icons.
- Not a clone of the reference channel.
- Not constant camera motion or overanimated backgrounds.
- Not detailed backgrounds that compete with the action.

## 10. Decision priorities

When choices conflict, prefer:
1. Story comprehension over visual complexity.
2. Original character identity over resemblance to a reference avatar.
3. Strong pose and timing over decorative effects.
4. Consistent graphic language over one-off spectacle.
5. Reviewable quality over raw throughput.
